\begin{abstract}
\noindent A public employment service offers a two-year retraining program and pays each assigned worker a flat allowance of €2,000, and a randomized pilot of the offer is the evidence for scaling it up. The pilot identifies an average earnings gain of €1,529 and, at an illustrative program cost of €3,000 per person, a marginal value of public funds of 0.68. The pilot does not identify how many workers end up below the earnings they would have had, because it never observes both earnings levels for the same person, and the share of workers whose disposable income falls after the allowance ranges from 2.6\% if the program leaves the ranking of workers almost intact to 26\% if it reshuffles that ranking and 71\% without restrictions. The same identification problem affects welfare measures that weight workers by their counterfactual earnings, whereas welfare changes computed from the two marginal distributions alone remain identified, and the marginal value of public funds with weights that favor the bottom of the counterfactual distribution ranges from 0.66 to 1.30 across the matchings the data allow while its unweighted counterpart is a single identified number. The evaluation reports the share of losers under declared scenarios, the divergence at which a distributional claim breaks, and the incidence on the most vulnerable cell, and it compares the allowance with a top-up of equal outlay that caps the share of losers under any matching at the price of weaker incentives to earn.\par
\vspace{4pt}\noindent\textit{Keywords}. Program evaluation, active labor market policy, distributional effects, partial identification, marginal value of public funds.\par
\noindent\textit{JEL codes}. D61, H43, J68.
\end{abstract}

\section{The policy question and the conceptual framework}
Retraining workers with low or unstable incomes is among the most widely funded active labor market policies in Europe, and the Italian recovery plan finances a national program of this kind, so whether such programs raise the earnings of the workers they reach, and at what fiscal cost, is a live question. The policy evaluated here is a two-year retraining program offered by a public employment service to workers with low or unstable incomes, with a flat allowance of €2,000 over the two years paid to those who attend. The program was piloted through a lottery of the offer among 6,000 eligible applicants, stratified by past income and contract type, and the pilot is the evidence on which the decision to scale it up rests. The paper asks three questions of that evidence, namely whether the program raises the earnings of the workers it reaches, what it delivers per euro of public money, and how many workers it leaves below the earnings they would have had without it, since a program that raises average earnings while pushing a minority into losses has distributional and political consequences of its own. As Fernández and Rodrik \cite{fernandezrodrik} show, uncertainty about who would lose from a reform can block reforms that most would support ex post, and the compensation principle studied by Schulz, Tsyvinski and Werquin \cite{schulz} presupposes that losers can be found, so the share of losers is treated here as an evaluative object on the same footing as the average effect.

The literature gives reasons for both signs, since training can raise earnings through transferable skills, better matching and shorter unemployment spells, while Card, Kluve and Weber \cite{card} document that such gains appear with a lag and depend on the horizon and on the target population, whereas in the short run training programs show lock-in effects, since participants search less and work fewer hours during the course, and a mismatch between the skills taught and local demand can leave some of them with lower earnings than they would have had. Over a 24-month window that partly overlaps with the course, the forgone earnings of a worker who declines job offers to attend can therefore exceed the allowance, and Bitler, Gelbach and Hoynes \cite{bitler} show that average effects can hide exactly such heterogeneity. The framework is thus a trade-off between the average earnings gain, its fiscal cost and the losses of a minority, and the open question that the evidence must address is which of the two forces dominates and for whom.

\section{Data and identification strategy}
The public employment service records assignment to the offer and attendance because it administers the lottery and pays the allowance, and earnings come from the monthly contribution records that employers file for each employee, which report pay and contract type because contributions are assessed on them, with tax returns for self-employment income and the unemployment insurance registers for benefit spells. The outcome is pre-tax labor earnings cumulated over the 24 months after assignment, excluding the allowance, so that the fiscal calculation can apply a tax rate to it, and a worker is counted as a loser when disposable income, that is after-tax earnings plus the tax-free allowance, falls below the after-tax earnings she would have had without the program. These records do not report hours, so a fall in earnings cannot be separated into fewer days worked and lower pay, and they miss informal work, a limit that returns in Section 4 when income-contingent transfers raise the return to underreporting.

The lottery makes the workers assigned to the offer comparable with the applicants who were not drawn, so the difference in cumulated earnings identifies the effect of the offer. In the synthetic pilot every assigned worker attends, so the effect of the offer and the effect of participation coincide, the program cost of €3,000 and the allowance are both paid per assigned person, and the estimate is €1,529 with an approximate 95\% interval from €1,270 to €1,789, against a population value of €1,575. The comparison can mislead in two ways, since retrained workers may find jobs at the expense of non-assigned applicants in the same local market, which Crépon, Duflo, Gurgand, Rathelot and Zamora \cite{crepon} document for job search assistance, so the pilot can overstate the gain that a scaled-up program would deliver. Workers who leave the registers, by moving abroad or into informal work, appear with zero recorded earnings, and if this happens more often in one arm the comparison is biased. Exhibit~\ref{fig:pilot} reports the estimates by cell for stable part-time employees, fixed-term workers and intermittent workers, with untreated mean earnings of €24,000, €18,000 and €12,000 over two years and dispersion growing in that order.

\section{Costs, benefits and distributional effects}
Costs and benefits are summarized with the marginal value of public funds proposed by Hendren and Sprung-Keyser \cite{hendren}, the ratio between the beneficiaries' willingness to pay for the policy and its net cost to the government. With a program cost of €3,000 per assigned person, a tax rate of 30\% on earnings gains and the allowance valued at face value, willingness to pay is €3,070 per person against a net cost of €4,541, an MVPF of 0.68 with a sampling interval from 0.63 to 0.73 (Exhibit~\ref{tab:eval}). Without the allowance the ratio falls to 0.42, because the allowance is a pure transfer whose own MVPF is one, and the ratio exceeds 0.85 only if the program costs €2,000 or less. None of these numbers involves the distribution of gains and losses across individuals, which is where the information in the pilot ends.

The pilot observes different people in the two arms, so it identifies the distribution of earnings with the program and the distribution without it, and it never observes both for the same person. The share of workers who end up below the earnings they would have had, even after the allowance, depends on how the two are matched, and no sample size settles that matching, as the bounds derived by Fan and Park \cite{fanpark} from the two distributions alone make clear. The evaluation therefore states two reference scenarios before looking at the results. In the persistent scenario the course leaves the ranking of workers almost intact, so that whoever would have earned more without it also earns more with it, with 10\% of workers reshuffled at random, and in the independent scenario the course reshuffles the ranking entirely. Under the persistent scenario 2.6\% of assigned workers lose despite the allowance, under the independent one 25.9\%, and with no restriction on the matching the share can reach 71.3\%. Each scenario is then allowed to be wrong by a measured amount, and the divergence of the truth from a scenario is reported as a share of the divergence of the independent scenario from the persistent one, so that every statement is relative to a gap the reader can picture.\footnote{The divergence between two matchings of the same earnings distributions is their Kullback-Leibler divergence, computed on a 25-point grid for each cell, and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(R_{\mathrm{ind}}\,\|\,R_{\mathrm{pers}})=2.09$ on that grid, where the unrestricted share of losers is 67.6\%. The divergence is asymmetric, so the normalization is a reading aid and never a linear interpolation between the two scenarios. Values at the scenarios and without restrictions are continuous closed forms, while values at a positive divergence are computed on the grid. The worst case within a given distance is a convex program, solved with the entropic transport methods of Léonard \cite{leonard} and Galichon and Henry \cite{galichon}, and Fan, Park and Xu \cite{fanparkxu} study the related problem of distributional model risk in marginal problems, with the marginals themselves in Wasserstein balls.} Exhibit~\ref{fig:losers} traces the worst-case share as that divergence grows. Under the persistent scenario the claim that at most 10\% lose survives as long as the divergence of the truth from that scenario stays below 5.8\% of the gap, and under the independent scenario the claim already fails at the scenario itself, while the weaker claim that at most half lose fails beyond 22\% of the same gap, in the spirit of the breakdown frontiers of Masten and Poirier \cite{masten}. With 5\% or 20\% reshuffled instead of 10\%, the share is 1.2\% or 4.9\% and the claim survives to 8.0\% or 2.7\% of the gap.

\exhibitblock{figures/exhibit_2_losers.pdf}{fig:losers}{\textbf{Worst-case share of losers as a function of the divergence from the reference scenario.} On the left, the worst-case share of workers whose disposable income falls after the €2,000 allowance, as the truth is allowed to diverge from each scenario, measured as a share of the divergence of the independent scenario from the persistent one. On the right, the worst-case share by cell at 5\% of that gap, when the entire allowed divergence may fall on one cell.}

At 5\% of the gap under the persistent scenario the worst-case shares by cell are 6.1\%, 10.1\% and 12.9\%, so the intermittent workers, with the lowest earnings and the widest dispersion, are the most exposed, and under the independent scenario the figures are 27.9\%, 40.0\% and 46.7\%. The same computation bounds the severity of losses, and at either scenario a loser falls short of her counterfactual disposable income by about €3,280 on average, while the population average shortfall rises from €85 under the persistent scenario to €396 at 5\% of the gap, from €852 to €1,310 under the independent one, and to €1,671 without restrictions, against an average after-tax gain of €1,103.

The marginal value of public funds is identified because it aggregates willingness to pay without weights, whereas the distributional version used here weights each person by her counterfactual earnings, and the product of a weight that depends on counterfactual earnings and a gain that depends on realized earnings depends on how the two are matched. With weights that are twice the average at the bottom of the counterfactual distribution and zero at the top, the weighted MVPF is 0.69 under the persistent scenario and 0.98 under the independent one, and across all matchings it ranges from 0.66 to 1.30, straddling the threshold of one that the unweighted measure places at 0.68 without ambiguity. At 5\% of the gap the range is from 0.66 to 0.80 around the persistent scenario and from 0.84 to 1.13 around the independent one, and with weights three times the average at the bottom the full range widens to 0.65 to 1.67 while the independent scenario alone crosses one at 1.16.

\section{Trade-offs and policy conclusion}
The flat allowance protects nobody in particular, since it raises every treated income by the same amount, and its distributional effect rests entirely on the unobserved matching. The same expected outlay spent as a top-up indexed to after-tax earnings, which raises each worker's disposable income to the 67th percentile of counterfactual after-tax earnings in the cell, caps the share of losers at 33.2\% in every cell under any matching, because only workers whose counterfactual earnings exceed the threshold can lose, and it leaves the shares under the two scenarios close to those of the allowance, at 24.2\% and 2.4\%, so that the same money buys a guarantee that holds without assumptions in place of one that holds conditionally, and the price is an implicit marginal tax rate of 100\% below the threshold, which weakens the incentive to earn and raises the return to underreporting income, the trade-off that Saez \cite{saez} analyzes for the optimal phase-out of income transfers. Verlaat, Todeschini and Ramos \cite{verlaat} find in the randomized evaluation of a guaranteed income in Barcelona that beneficiaries were 22\% less likely to work after two years and that a slower phase-out attenuates the effect, while Hyman, Kovak and Leive \cite{hyman} find that a wage insurance paid only to re-employed workers raises employment, so the direction of the behavioral response depends on whether the transfer is conditioned on working, and the comparison is between an assumption the data cannot test and a behavioral cost that only a second randomized arm offering the top-up could measure.

The evidence supports with confidence a positive average earnings gain and an MVPF below one at a program cost of €3,000, and since the allowance enters benefits and net costs by the same amount the ratio reaches one only when the program cost falls to the average gain itself, €1,529, so scaling up on efficiency grounds alone requires either that cost or a larger gain than the pilot found. The claim that few workers lose holds under the persistent scenario and within a small distance of it, and it cannot be reported as a finding of the pilot, nor can the verdict of a welfare measure weighted by counterfactual earnings, so an evaluation of this policy should publish the share of losers and the weighted welfare measure under at least two declared scenarios together with the distance at which its claim breaks, and should state the incidence on the most vulnerable cell, which the aggregate share hides. Three limitations are specific to this design, the first being that the synthetic earnings are uniform within cells, without zero earners or a long right tail, and both features could materially change the thresholds and the shortfalls. The 24-month horizon lies partly inside the lock-in period, so some losers at 24 months may be winners at 48, which a longer follow-up in the same registers can establish. Displacement is invisible in a lottery among individuals and calls for randomizing the intensity of the offer across local markets before a roll-out. Where protection is part of the policy's purpose, the top-up comparison at equal outlay belongs in the evaluation as a measured trade-off between robustness and incentives.
